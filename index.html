              1. Data Collection & Curation Pipeline
            

            

            

              An AI model is only as good as the data it reads. Before any neural network exists, petabytes of raw web pages, code, books, and papers are harvested, deduplicated, scrubbed of personal data, and filtered for quality.
            

          


          

            
            
            
          

        


        
        

          

            

              Picture it: washing and sorting ingredients before cooking. If spoiled or repeated ingredients go in the pot, even the best chef cooks a bad meal.
            

          

          
          
        


        
        

          

            

              
What goes in

              
Raw crawls (CommonCrawl), code repos, articles, books

            

          

          

            

              
What happens

              
HTML extraction, MinHash deduplication, PII redaction, quality scoring

            

          

          

            

              
What comes out

              
Clean, high-quality training text (1 to 15 trillion word pieces)

                Part 2 of 5 • Attention
              

              
Attention: Which Words Matter?

              

                Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(Q·Kᵀ / √d_k + Mask) · V
              

              

                Each token emits a Query (Q) ("what am I looking for?"), a Key (K) ("what do I contain?"), and a Value (V) ("what do I pass along?"). Matching each question to the labels tells the model which words to pay attention to.

              Go deeper 4B
              Three Ways to Split One Giant Model
            

            

              

                
1. Split each calculation (8 chips in one machine)

                
Cuts each big calculation into slices (WQ, WK, WV, WMLP) shared by 8 chips joined with very fast cables (900 GB/s).

              

              

                
2. Split by layers (4 machines)

                
Different machines handle different layers, like stations on an assembly line (for example layers 1 to 20 on machine 1, and 21 to 40 on machine 2).

              

              

                
3. Many copies, different data (128 copies)

                
Each copy reads different text, then they all share what they learned.

Three ways to teach preferences:

              

                
                
                
              

              

                Reward model + practice: First trains a separate judge model (the reward model) Rφ(x, y) from human comparisons. Then the main model practices to earn high scores from the judge, with a penalty for changing too much so it cannot cheat the judge.

              Go deeper 8B
              Two Phases: Reading Your Question, Then Writing the Answer
            

            

              

                
Phase 1: Reading your question (limited by calculation speed)

                
Reads all N pieces of your question at the same time. This keeps the chip busy and decides how long you wait for the first word to appear.

              

              

                
Phase 2: Writing the answer (limited by memory speed)

                
Writes one piece at a time. For every piece, the chip must fetch all the model's numbers from memory. This decides the pause between words (how fast they stream in).

Basics: How a Machine Learns from Examples

        
Three ideas sit under every AI model: learning without memorizing, numbers as arrows, and teaching by example. Guess first, then play.

      

      

        
      

      

        

          
1. Learn, Don't Memorize (learning vs. memorizing)

          

          
The model fits a curve through the blue training points. The orange circles are new data it has never seen. A good model does well on both.

          

            
            
          

          
Training examples:
            

          
          

          
          
Where is this used in AI?
AI models have billions of settings, enough to memorize their training data. To stop that, they are tested on data they have not seen, trained on more data, and kept simple with tricks like weight decay and dropout. What matters is the error on new data, not on the training data.

        


        

          
2. Numbers as Arrows (similarity and probabilities)

          

          
A model stores each word as a list of numbers, which you can picture as an arrow. Arrows pointing the same way mean similar things.

          
Try:
            

          

            
            
            
          

          
          

          

            
Scores turned into percentages. The model gives each possible next word a score. Softmax turns the scores into chances that add up to 100%.

            

              
              
              
            

            

          

          
Where is this used in AI?
Real models store words as lists of hundreds or thousands of numbers, like much longer arrows. They compare them to see which words relate, and search tools find similar text the same way. Softmax turns the final scores into next-word chances, which the Randomness page then picks from.

        


        

          
3. Teach with a Prompt (learning from examples in the question)

          

          
You can guide a trained model by putting worked examples in your question. Nothing is retrained. The replies below are scripted to show the idea, not from a live model.

          
Task:

Where is this used in AI?
This is called "few-shot prompting". The model does not change. It simply reads your examples as part of the question and copies the pattern. Longer questions use more of its short-term memory, so every example has a cost.

Randomness: From Random Walks to Diffusion and LLM Sampling

            
Every AI model runs on controlled randomness. Guess first, play with each demo, then check your guess. Ideas from The Nature of Code, Ch. 0: Randomness.

          

          

            
            
            
          

        

        
Computers do not roll real dice. They use a formula that only looks random: same seed, same "random" numbers. Change the seed to re-roll every demo.

      


      

        
        
        
        
      

      


        
        

          
1. Random Walks (a walker taking random steps)

          

          
Pick a step rule and watch the walker.

          

            
            
            
            
          

          
          

          
Where is this used in AI?
Learning in AI is a bumpy random walk through the model's settings. Image generators and many other methods are random walks too.

        


        
        

          
2. From Noise to Pictures (Diffusion) (bell-curve noise)

          

          
Drag the slider and watch a shape dissolve into noise, the way image generators are trained.

          

            
            
            
          

          
          

          
Where is this used in AI?
The network learns to remove the noise that was added. To make a new image, it starts from pure noise and removes it step by step.

        


        
        

          
3. Choosing the Starting Settings (how spread out the random numbers are)

          

          
Try:

          
Send a random signal through 20 layers. If the starting numbers are too small or too big, the signal fades away or blows up.

          

            
            
            
          

          
          

          
Where is this used in AI?
Xavier and He pick the spread from the layer width, so the signal stays steady in deep networks.

        


        
        

          
4. Getting the Odds You Want (Accept or Reject) (trial and error with random numbers)

          

          
Keep a random r1 only if a second random r2 is below r1k.

          

            
            
            
          

          
          

          
Where is this used in AI?
Speeding up AI answers uses this idea: a small model's guess is accepted with a certain chance. Picking the best of several tries is a close cousin.

        


        
        

          
5. How an AI Picks the Next Word (a weighted dice roll)

          

          
Try:

          
Start of the story: "The cat sat on the ___". Change the scores, then a random number picks one slice of the strip.

          

            
            
            
          

          

          

            
Chance strip: the black marker shows the random number used for the last pick.

            

          

          

            
            
            
          

          
Where is this used in AI?
Low temperature gives predictable answers; high gives creative ones. Top-k and top-p cut off the very unlikely words. Every reply is thousands of these picks.

        


        
        

          
6. Smooth vs. Jagged Randomness (smooth noise)

          

          
Jagged random numbers versus smooth ones. Bottom: layered smooth noise, like clouds or terrain.

          

            
            
          

          
          
          

          
Where is this used in AI?
Real data is smooth: nearby points are similar. Gliding smoothly through an AI's 'space of ideas' morphs one image into another, while random jumps give garbage.

        


        
        

          
7. Multiplying Probabilities: Why Long Answers Go Wrong (basic probability)

          

          
Three heads in a row is (1/2)3 = 12.5%. An answer is a chain of word-piece choices.

          

            
            
          

          

          

          
Where is this used in AI?
The chance of a whole answer is every piece's chance multiplied together, so long answers drift. Tests such as pass@k estimate this with many random trials.

Neural Networks: Learning by Adjusting Dials

        
A neural network is many tiny decision-makers (neurons) that each weigh their inputs. Learning means nudging those weights, like dials, to make fewer mistakes. Ideas from The Nature of Code, Ch. 10: Neural Networks. The random starting dials come from the Randomness page.

      

      

        
        
      

      

        

          
1. One Neuron (inputs, weights, bias and an on/off rule)

          

          
Click the plot to move the input. Drag the sliders to change the neuron.

          

            
            
            
          

          
Activation:
            

          
          

          
Where is this used in AI?
Every layer of every AI model is made of many of these neurons working together. Their weights and biases are the model's 'settings': 7 billion of them in a 7B model.

        


        

          
2. Teach a Neuron (learning from mistakes)

          

          
Start with random weights. Each mistake nudges them: new weight = old weight + mistake × input × learning speed.

          

            
            
            
            
          

          
          
          

          
Where is this used in AI?
This is how machines learn from examples: guess, measure the mistake, adjust. Modern models use fancier methods, but the loop is the same.

Where is this used in AI?
A middle layer lets the network draw curved boundaries, not just straight lines. Training repeats: guess, measure the mistake, send it backward, adjust. This is step 4 (learning from lots of text) at a tiny scale. Transformers stack many such layers.
